Philosophy: Customer Focus

I have always been customer focused because customers giving the company money funds my paycheck. No customers paying us means no paycheck.

I work with a lot of people who seem to think working is just a game — they show up for 8 hours, leave, and magically money appears in their bank account. That’s not how it works.

Make sure whatever you’re doing achieves one of these:

  1. It’s a feature or product customers value enough to pay money for.
  2. It reduces the chance of bad things happening so customers continue to pay you money and not your competitors.
  3. When something bad happens, you do anything needed to restore their confidence in your product.
“It's not quite putting a man on the moon but it's close.“

This summarized the types of products that I've worked on through my entire career. I've learned the hard way that mistakes are costly.

  1. If customers cannot access data, it can cost a million dollars an hour. Do not let that happen.
  2. I've been on escalations where 20,000 people at a company can't get work done because a bug was hit.
  3. If Yahoo fantasy football stops working, then a lot of people are going to lose money.
  4. If ILM can't render a Star Wars movie, or DreamWorks’ rendering farm is down, very important people are going to be very upset.

Development Guidelines for Successful Products

General principles for building quality products.

“If you can’t explain <feature> in 1 sentence without using the terms ‘except’ or ‘but’, think hard about what you are doing.”

The inability to simply describe what you are doing indicates that it is overly complicated, or you really do not understand what you intend to implement.

“If it’s not tested it doesn’t work.”

Don’t make assumptions that simple code is obviously correct because it’s not. You need to prove that something works and figure out under what conditions it fails.

“Technology is a tool not a product. Customers care about outcomes, not how they were achieved.”

Something that looks neat comes out and all the engineers want to play with it, so they justify its use in production — which is almost never a good idea.

“Complexity masks fragility.”

Always pick the simplest solution that solves the problem you need to solve, to minimize the error paths.

“No single person is good enough to come up with the best solution for complicated products.”

We work in teams for a reason — everyone has different experiences that give them different perspectives. Write up your solution and have it reviewed before you start implementation. It’s far too common to get a code review that has taken the wrong approach, only to hear “this works and we don’t have time to rework it.”

“Every line of code you write is a future liability.”

Completing a feature in 500 lines that could have been written in 50 is how technical debt builds up.
